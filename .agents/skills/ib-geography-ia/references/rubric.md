# Geography IA marking reference

Sources include the IB *Geography guide*, first assessment 2019, updated November 2022, pp. 69–80, and the [IB Geography examiner instructions](https://ibpublishing.ibo.org/exinst/apps/exinst/index.html?chapter=4&doc=EX_instructions_2027_e&part=10).

Apply each criterion independently. Compare adjacent descriptors if the evidence is mixed. For bands with two marks, select the upper mark when the work demonstrates the descriptor's qualities to a greater extent. Top marks allow minor imperfections. Each criterion has a 0 level for work that fails to reach its lowest descriptor. For a confirmed overlength report, award marks only on the evidence up to the 2,500-word cutoff. See the [writing guide](writing.md) for counting rules.

## A. Fieldwork question and geographic context, /3

- **1:** The inquiry or syllabus/theory link is poorly formulated. The scale, location or primary data feasibility is unclear. A suitable map is absent.
- **2:** A geographical question is feasible at a specific site and linked to a relevant syllabus topic or theory. The location map may have extraneous detail or incomplete conventions.
- **3:** A focused, geographically grounded question identifies a precise location suitable for primary fieldwork. The theory or syllabus link is explained and can inform predictions where appropriate. A clear locational map uses appropriate conventions.

## B. Methods of investigation, /3

- **1:** Methods, tools or sampling are listed vaguely or used incorrectly. The plan yields insufficient relevant information.
- **2:** Methods and sampling are described and correctly applied. The link to the question or hypotheses is outlined, with sufficient but possibly limited data or variables.
- **3:** The description explains how the combination of methods and sampling tests the question, theory or hypotheses. Correct use yields reliable, relevant primary information of sufficient quality for the intended analysis.

## C. Quality and treatment of information collected, /6

- **1–2:** Much of the data is irrelevant or insufficient. Presentation makes analysis difficult or frequently breaks conventions.
- **3–4:** Most data is relevant and supports partial analysis. Displays suit the data and mostly follow conventions, with occasional errors.
- **5–6:** Directly relevant data is sufficient in quality and quantity. An appropriate range of techniques is used effectively. Titles, labels, figure numbers, units and other applicable conventions are accurate.

## D. Written analysis, /8

- **1–2:** Obvious patterns are listed. Techniques may be unsuitable, and links to the question or hypotheses are implicit.
- **3–4:** Appropriate descriptive techniques support a descriptive answer. Trends and patterns are explicitly tied to the question or hypotheses. Statistical techniques, if used, may be irrelevant or flawed.
- **5–6:** Appropriate techniques, including statistics where relevant, support an answer with some evidence gaps. Trends and results are linked explicitly to the question or hypotheses, and anomalies are identified where present. Look for explanation beyond simple description, as emphasized in the examiner instructions.
- **7–8:** Patterns, statistics and any anomalies are explained in relation to the question, relevant hypotheses, theory, site and methods. Descriptive and statistical tools, including confidence levels where appropriate, are correctly applied. The analysis supports the answer with few gaps.

## E. Conclusion, /2

- **1:** Answers the fieldwork question, with partial support from the analysis.
- **2:** Gives a clear answer supported by results already analyzed. It can confirm, qualify or reject an initial prediction where one was made.

## F. Evaluation, /3

- **1:** Lists mainly superficial or unsuitable strengths, weaknesses or improvements.
- **2:** Outlines mostly relevant strengths or weaknesses of data collection and suitable improvements.
- **3:** Explains relevant strengths or weaknesses of data collection, question formulation, presentation and site choice. Connects feasible improvements to their likely effect on the results or interpretation.

Check C and D independently. A technically accurate map or graph contributes to C. The geographical interpretation the student makes from it is evidence for D. In F, look for the direction or consequence of a limitation and how a proposed change would reduce it. For example, comparing sites at different times may confound site differences with time-of-day effects. Sampling the sites in comparable time windows could improve the comparison.
